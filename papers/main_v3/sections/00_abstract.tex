Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed for chest X-ray (CXR) interpretation, yet whether their apparent reliability is grounded in visual evidence, and how that grounding degrades under image-quality shift, remains poorly characterised. We evaluate seven open-source CXR VLMs spanning six model families on structured multi-label finding prediction, using a stratified NIH ChestX-ray14 pilot (1,000 studies for two primary models and a 100-study pre-run across all seven), a six-axis image-quality shift benchmark, counterfactual image-reliance tests, and a CheXpert-plus-RRG external report set.

Three findings emerge. First, visual reliance is not binary: across the seven models it forms a three-tier gradient with the visual-reliance signal falling from 0.36 to 0.00, spanning image-dependent (InternVL3.5, MedGemma-4B), partially dependent (Qwen3-VL, MedGemma-1.5), and image-blind (Qwen2.5-VL, CheXagent) models; an image-blind model can nonetheless emit structurally valid JSON and apparently favourable selective-prediction metrics. Second, degradation does not erode macro accuracy --- it selectively suppresses the positive recall of pathology labels, and this dose-response is degradation-type-specific, with reduced resolution and added noise the dominant factors while blur, brightness, and contrast leave recall largely unchanged. This accuracy paradox follows from label imbalance and implies that robustness benchmarks must report sensitivity rather than accuracy to escape the majority class. Third, the models separate into three distinct safety-failure modes in the absence of any parse error: default-normal (Qwen2.5-VL), refusal-to-answer (CheXagent), and prose-without-the-structured-contract (LLaVA-Med).

These results show that accuracy, calibration, or selective risk alone mischaracterise CXR VLM trustworthiness, and that reliability must be assessed jointly through visual grounding, positive recall, and failure mode. We contribute two instruments that make this assessment practical and cheap: a label-free, encoder-free visual-reliance signal computed from a model's own counterfactual output disagreement, and a label-level, recall-first reporting protocol that escapes the majority-class trap. We describe a shift-aware selective trust framework that conditions accept/abstain decisions on these signals; at present it is a conceptual blueprint grounded in the empirical findings, whose components remain to be ablated and validated in a full multi-centre run.
